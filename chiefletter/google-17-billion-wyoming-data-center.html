Google commits $17 billion to first Wyoming data center

The Laramie County campus will be air-cooled and run on a 702 MW fuel-cell plant with carbon capture.

By ChiefLetter StaffPublished Nov 5, 2026Updated Nov 12, 20264 min read

Key points

  • $17 billion investment in Laramie County, near Cheyenne, announced November 5.
  • Power comes from a 702 MW natural-gas fuel-cell project with carbon capture and storage.
  • The campus will be air-cooled to limit water use; Google says it will pay for 100% of the power it uses.
  • Google expects hundreds of millions of dollars in local property taxes, plus workforce and education grants.

Google said on November 5 that it will invest $17 billion in its first data center in Wyoming, a campus in Laramie County near Cheyenne. The company calls it a long-term commitment and says it plans to be in the state "for decades to come." The announcement names a group of energy and development partners: Tallgrass Energy, Black Hills Energy, American Electric Power (AEP) and Related Digital.

The most distinctive part of the plan is how the campus will be powered. Rather than relying only on the existing grid, Google has contracted through AEP for a 702 MW fuel-cell development built by Bloom Energy. Fuel cells convert natural gas into electricity through an electrochemical reaction instead of combustion, and Google says the project is the largest of its kind planned. Most of the carbon dioxide produced will be captured and stored permanently at Tallgrass's Eastern Wyoming Sequestration Hub.

How the power plan works

Alongside the fuel cells, the campus will draw on gas-fired generation operated by Tallgrass Energy and Black Hills Energy. Google also said a front-end engineering and design (FEED) study will evaluate adding carbon capture to Tallgrass's combined-cycle turbines, which would extend the same approach to more of the supply.

With this project, Google's contracted carbon capture capacity reaches nearly one gigawatt, building on the first U.S. carbon capture project it announced the previous fall. The company's message is that firm, around-the-clock power for AI workloads does not have to come at the expense of emissions, although the approach depends on carbon capture and storage performing at scale, something the industry is still proving.

On the cost question that increasingly dominates local debates, Google says it "will pay for 100% of the power we use" and will cover infrastructure needs directly driven by its growth. That pledge is aimed squarely at concerns that residential customers could end up subsidizing hyperscale demand through higher bills.

Water, taxes and the local deal

The campus will be air-cooled, which reduces the volume of water drawn from local supplies compared with evaporative cooling. Water use has become one of the most contested issues in data center siting, particularly in arid western states, so the cooling design is a central part of Google's pitch.

Google expects the project to generate "hundreds of millions of dollars" in property taxes for Laramie County and School District 1. Its community plans focus on K-12 education, workforce development and routes into skilled trades, including targeted grants for STEM programs and local entrepreneurs. The company held a first Community Day at the Cheyenne Children's Museum, met more than 200 residents at a downtown community meeting, and is setting up a Community Council to help shape how Cheyenne handles data-center-driven growth. It said more partnership details will follow "in the coming weeks."

The announcement does not give construction or completion dates, and it emphasizes tax revenue and training programs rather than a headline figure for permanent jobs. Data centers are capital-intensive but staff-light once built, which is one reason local officials often ask for firm employment commitments.

Why it matters

The timing is notable. The announcement landed in the same week voters in several states rejected or restricted data center projects at the ballot box, and as Microsoft reportedly declined to expand a Texas deal with an AI cloud provider. Google's package reads as an attempt to answer the objections that have driven that backlash: who pays for the power, how much water is used, and what the community gets in return.

For Wyoming, which has long marketed cheap land, cool air and a permissive energy policy to attract large users, the project is among the largest private investments the state has seen. For the industry, it is a test case for whether gas-plus-carbon-capture can be a credible template for power-hungry AI campuses.

What to watch next

  • Permitting and regulatory approvals for the fuel-cell plant and the carbon storage infrastructure.
  • Construction timeline and the first details of the Community Council's work.
  • Whether other hyperscalers copy the "pay for all the power we use" commitment.
  • Evidence on how effectively captured carbon is stored and monitored over time.
