Two-factor authentication methods ranked from weakest to strongest

Not all second factors protect you equally. This guide compares text codes, authenticator apps, push approvals and hardware keys.

Why a second factor matters

Two-factor authentication, often shortened to 2FA, asks for something beyond your password, such as a code or a physical device. Even if a password leaks, an attacker still needs the second factor. Any form of 2FA is better than none, but the methods differ in how well they stop determined attackers.

Text message codes

SMS codes are the most widely supported and the easiest to use, which makes them a reasonable starting point. Their weakness is that phone numbers can be hijacked through SIM swapping, and codes can be intercepted or phished. Use them only when nothing better is offered.

Authenticator apps and push approvals

Authenticator apps generate short-lived codes on your device, so nothing travels over the phone network. They are stronger than SMS, but a fake login page can still trick you into typing the code in real time.

Push approvals are convenient, yet users sometimes approve prompts they did not start. Enable number matching where it is offered, and never approve a request you did not initiate.

Hardware security keys and passkeys

Physical security keys and passkeys are the strongest consumer options because they verify the real website before responding, which defeats phishing pages. They require a little setup and a spare key for backup.

  • Best: hardware key or passkey.
  • Good: authenticator app with number matching.
  • Acceptable: SMS code when nothing else exists.
  • Avoid: relying on a single factor for email or banking.

A practical plan

Secure your primary email and financial accounts first, using the strongest method each supports. Store backup codes offline, and review your recovery options every year so an old phone number or email does not become a back door.

Setting up 2FA on your three most important accounts

Start with your primary email, your main financial account and your phone carrier account. These are the accounts attackers want most, because email resets other passwords, banks hold money and carriers control your phone number. For each, open the security settings and look for two-step verification. Choose a passkey or security key if offered, an authenticator app otherwise, and SMS only as a last resort.

When you enable 2FA, the service usually shows backup codes. Treat them like spare house keys: print them or write them down and store them somewhere safe and separate from your phone. Then test your recovery path by confirming you could regain access if your phone were lost tonight. Finally, add a PIN or passcode with your mobile carrier to make SIM-swap attempts harder. These steps take under an hour in total and close the gaps behind most account takeovers.

Frequently asked questions

Is SMS two-factor still worth using?

Yes, it is far better than a password alone, but switch to an authenticator app, passkey or security key when a service allows it.

What are backup codes?

One-time codes you receive when enabling 2FA. Store them offline so you can recover your account if you lose your phone.

Do authenticator apps need internet?

No. They generate codes from a shared secret and the current time, so they work offline.

What is SIM swapping?

A scam where someone convinces a mobile carrier to move your number to their SIM, letting them receive your text codes. Adding a carrier PIN helps.

Key terms

2FA
A login that needs two kinds of proof.
TOTP
Time-based one-time passcodes generated by an app.
FIDO2
The standard behind security keys and passkeys.
The bottom line. Turn on the strongest second factor each important account supports, save backup codes offline, and review recovery options each year.
