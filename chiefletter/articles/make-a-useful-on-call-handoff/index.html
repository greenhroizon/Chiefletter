Write an On-Call Handoff the Next Person Can Use

Hand over service status, open risks, and next actions in a format that helps the next engineer take over safely.

State what is happening now

A handoff should begin with the current service state, not a timeline of every message in the incident room. Name the affected service, user-visible impact, start time with a time zone, and whether the issue is ongoing. If the service recovered, say when and what evidence supports that conclusion.

Separate confirmed facts from working theories. “Error rate rose from 1% to 12% after the release” is an observation. “The cache change caused it” is a hypothesis until evidence connects the two. Labeling the difference prevents the next person from inheriting a guess as if it were a diagnosis.

Give the next engineer a short first action. For example, “Check the payment provider status page and our latest timeout graph before restarting workers.” A handoff that says only “keep an eye on it” leaves the person to reconstruct what matters and what a meaningful change would look like.

A concise handoff might read: “Checkout errors began at 14:10 UTC. The rate is now 4%, down from 12% after disabling the new retry path. Keep the flag off. Check the payment provider status and our timeout graph at 15:00 UTC. Page the service owner if errors exceed 8% for five minutes. Priya owns the next customer update.” It gives state, action, threshold, and owner.

Include links with enough context

Link to the active incident channel, dashboard, runbook, recent deployment, and relevant ticket. Add a short label that says what the link contains. “Latency chart for checkout, last six hours” is more helpful than a collection of raw URLs. Check that the next person has permission before relying on a private dashboard.

Prefer one source of truth for updates. If the same status is copied across chat, email, and a document, someone will eventually read an outdated version. State where the current update lives and keep the handoff itself short enough to scan during a busy shift.

Avoid placing credentials, customer records, or sensitive incident details in a general handoff. Link to an approved system with the right access controls. Redact customer identifiers in broad channels. A handoff should help the team respond while respecting the people affected by the incident.

Record actions already taken

List changes made, commands run, and their results. “Restarted worker pool at 14:20 UTC; queue returned to normal for three minutes, then backlog rose” gives the next person evidence. “Restarted it, did not help” hides timing and the behavior needed to judge whether another action is safe.

Mention any temporary mitigation and its cost. A feature flag may reduce errors but leave some users without a feature. A larger instance may restore response time while raising cost. The receiving engineer needs to know what has been traded away and who approved the mitigation.

Include actions to avoid. If a database migration is running or a rollback would lose writes, state that clearly. Do not assume the person on the next shift remembers the details from a long incident call. A concise warning near the relevant next step is easier to act on than a buried transcript.

Make open risks and thresholds clear

Say what would make the situation urgent again. A defined signal such as “page the database owner if replica lag exceeds 30 seconds for five minutes” is actionable. “Escalate if it looks bad” forces the next engineer to invent a threshold while the service is under stress.

Name the people or roles to contact and the appropriate channel. Confirm whether they are already involved, asleep, or waiting for a specific update. Follow the team’s paging policy. A handoff should not encourage unnecessary direct messages when the incident process has a clear owner.

State what is unknown and how to resolve it. “We have not confirmed whether older mobile clients are affected. The next check is the version breakdown in dashboard X” is honest and gives the next person a path. Unknowns are easier to manage when they have an owner and a next observation.

End with ownership and timing

List the next actions with one owner and a target time for each. If a task has no owner, say so and assign it during the handoff. A list of five urgent actions with no names is a wish list, not a plan. Choose the work that protects users first and defer cleanup that can wait.

Confirm that the receiving engineer has accepted the handoff and can access the linked systems. If they need a second person to approve a risky change, name that dependency. A message posted at shift end does not transfer responsibility by itself; the team should use its explicit acknowledgement process.

For an incident that may affect customers, include the next customer update time and its owner. Do not promise a resolution time you cannot support. A clear update schedule reduces repeated questions and gives the team a moment to reassess the evidence.

If the incoming person spots a conflict in the notes, pause the transfer and resolve the disagreement while both people are present. A verbal acknowledgement is useful when followed by a written update in the shared incident record. That gives later responders one current status to trust.

Close the outgoing shift only after the acknowledgement and current status appear in the agreed record. If the receiving person cannot take ownership, use the escalation route rather than leaving an alert unattended. A clear transfer protects the service and the people who respond to it.

Write for a tired person at an odd hour

Use short headings and plain language. Expand an uncommon acronym once. Put the alert state and next action near the top. A person taking over at night should not have to read a page of background before they find out whether the service is still failing.

Use absolute timestamps with a time zone. “At 02:10” is ambiguous when the team spans regions or daylight-saving changes. Technical logs should use a consistent standard; the handoff can show a local time as well when the recipient needs it.

After the incident, turn durable lessons into the runbook or monitoring system. The handoff is temporary. Update the source instructions when a threshold, dashboard, or recovery step changes. This keeps the next rotation from repeating the same discovery under pressure.

Adapt the fields to your team’s incident policy. A handoff template should not force every event into the same severity or require an engineer to paste private customer data. Keep the form short, review it after real incidents, and remove questions that nobody uses. A template earns trust when it saves time during a difficult shift.

Put the advice to work

Include current impact, the last confirmed healthy time, what changed, the next check, and who owns the update. Link to the dashboard and runbook rather than pasting a long discussion. State what remains uncertain. The incoming person should know the next action before reading the full incident history.

Frequently asked questions

What should an on-call handoff include?

Current impact, confirmed facts, actions and results, open risks, links, owners, next steps, escalation thresholds, and the receiving person’s acknowledgement.

How long should handoff notes be?

Short enough to scan and detailed enough to act. Put current state and the next action first, then link to the full incident record.

Should I include theories about the cause?

Yes, if you label them as hypotheses and state what evidence would confirm or reject them.

When is the handoff complete?

When the next person accepts ownership, has access to the needed systems, and understands the next actions and escalation rules.
