A Practical Plan for Technical Interviews

Prepare for technical interviews with a repeatable practice loop, calmer problem solving, and better questions for the people interviewing you.

Prepare for technical interviews with a repeatable practice loop, calmer problem solving, and better questions for the people interviewing you. This is a practical field note for people trying to make progress in technology without pretending the work is always straightforward. The useful answer is rarely a magic shortcut; it is a sequence of choices you can understand, test, and adjust.

Find out what kind of interview this is

“Technical interview” can mean a live coding exercise, a systems discussion, a take-home project, a debugging session, or a conversation about past work. Preparing for all of them at once is a reliable way to feel unprepared. Ask the recruiter what format to expect, how long it will take, what tools you may use, and whether the role has a particular language or domain focus.

A reasonable employer should be able to describe the process at a high level. You do not need secret questions; you need enough context to prepare appropriately. If there are several rounds, write down the format and the skill each round is likely to explore. Then match practice to the task rather than collecting a giant list of topics.

Practice explaining, not just solving

During a coding problem, narrate the decisions that matter. Restate the problem, identify assumptions, describe a straightforward approach, then discuss how you would improve it. If you find a counterexample, say so and revise. Interviewers are trying to understand your reasoning as well as your final answer, and silence makes even good thinking hard to assess.

Practice with a timer, but do not turn every session into a race. First solve problems without a clock and write down the pattern you noticed. Later, add a time limit and practice recovering when your first idea fails. A useful review asks where you lost time: understanding the prompt, choosing a data structure, handling an edge case, or explaining your work.

Build a compact practice routine

For two or three weeks, try a manageable loop: one focused problem on a study day, a review of yesterday’s solution, and a short explanation spoken aloud. Rotate through the areas relevant to the role rather than trying to master every topic that appears in interview books. A backend role may call for data modeling and API trade-offs; a frontend role may involve browser behavior and accessibility.

Keep an error log. Note the problem type, the mistaken assumption, and the signal that would help you catch it next time. Do not save a folder full of copied solutions that you never revisit. Retrieval is what makes a concept available under pressure. Re-solve selected problems from a blank page several days later and explain why the approach works.

Use a steady process when you get stuck

When the answer is not coming, make the uncertainty specific. Ask a clarifying question, walk through a small example, or propose the simplest correct approach before optimizing. If you have been stuck on one idea for several minutes, communicate that and test a smaller case. A thoughtful partial solution gives the interviewer something to respond to.

If the interviewer offers a hint, treat it as collaboration. Repeat the hint in your own words, then incorporate it into the solution. Do not spend the rest of the interview trying to prove you would have found it alone. In the debrief, write down what the hint revealed about your understanding. That is the material for the next practice session.

Interview the team, too

Your questions should help you understand the daily work. Ask how a change moves from idea to production, what a new team member typically owns in the first few months, how the team handles incidents, or what the role needs to improve this year. These questions invite concrete answers and help you see whether the team’s expectations match the job description.

Listen for specifics. “We value collaboration” is a pleasant phrase; an example of how design and engineering resolve a disagreement is more revealing. You do not have to interrogate anyone. A couple of thoughtful questions can help you choose well and make the conversation feel less like an exam.

Put it into practice

Choose one idea from this guide and give it a small, real test this week. Set aside a specific block of time, decide what you will try, and write down what happened. If the first attempt does not work, keep the result: it tells you something about the problem, your assumptions, or the way you approached it. Progress gets easier to see when the experiment is small enough to finish.

For a useful next step, explain the idea to a colleague, friend, or your future self in a few sentences. Notice which parts you can describe clearly and which parts still feel vague. That is a better measure of understanding than simply rereading the article or saving another link for later.

There will be details that depend on your tools, employer, or location. Use the official documentation and current guidance for those specifics. The broader habit is durable: be clear about the problem, make your assumptions visible, and look at the evidence before deciding what to do next.

Good progress is usually less dramatic than a breakthrough. It is the next useful thing you can repeat.

You do not need to do every step at once. Pick the part that removes the most friction, practice it, and return when you are ready for the next layer. The work is allowed to be gradual.

A small field exercise

To put “A Practical Plan for Technical Interviews” to work, choose a recent situation where this question came up for you. Write down the context in a few plain sentences: what you were trying to do, what got in the way, and what a useful outcome would look like. Keep the first description factual. It is easier to make a good decision when the problem is visible on the page instead of circling around as a general feeling.

Next, revisit the idea in “Find out what kind of interview this is.” Ask what information you already have and what you are assuming. Mark the difference. If an important fact is missing, decide how to find it safely: check the relevant documentation, ask someone with context, or run a small experiment. A ten-minute check can save a long detour built around a guess.

Use “Practice explaining, not just solving” to choose one action that fits the situation. Make the action small enough to complete in one sitting and clear enough that you will know whether it helped. Write down what you expect to happen before you begin. That prediction gives you something to compare with the result and makes a surprising outcome useful rather than discouraging.

Afterward, record three things: what changed, what did not change, and what you would do differently next time. Include details that another person could understand. If the result depends on a tool, employer, or local rule, note which version or setting you used. Good notes keep you from repeating the same investigation and make it easier to explain your decisions to a teammate, mentor, or interviewer.

Finally, decide whether the next step is worth taking now. You may have enough evidence to continue, or you may have found a new question that needs attention first. Either answer is progress. The point is not to force a success story from every attempt; it is to make the next choice with a little more information than you had before.

Frequently asked questions

How many coding problems should I solve before an interview?

There is no useful universal number. A smaller set reviewed carefully is often more helpful than many problems completed once. Prioritize patterns that match the role and practice explaining your reasoning.

What if I do not know the answer in an interview?

Say what you do know, ask a clarifying question, and work through a small example. Interviewers can learn from your reasoning even when you need a hint.

Should I memorize solutions?

Memorize core concepts and common patterns, not a script for specific prompts. A memorized answer is brittle when the problem changes slightly.

How do I reduce interview anxiety?

Practice the interview format with another person, prepare the logistics the day before, and use a repeatable opening routine. Anxiety may not disappear, but familiarity can make it less disruptive.
