How to Write a Tech Resume When You Have No Experience

Turn coursework, projects, and previous work into clear evidence without inventing experience or stuffing the page with keywords.

BY CHIEFLETTER  ·  November 12, 2026  ·  8 MIN READ

Begin with the work you can prove

A blank experience section can make a resume feel impossible. It is not a reason to invent a job title. Think instead about the work you have done that demonstrates relevant behavior: a personal project, a course assignment you extended, a volunteer website, a process you improved in another role, or a difficult customer problem you resolved. The point is to make the evidence easy to see.

Write a list before you format anything. For each item, note the situation, what you did, tools you used, and the result. A result does not have to be a giant percentage. “Reduced repeated data entry by replacing a paper form with a shared spreadsheet” is specific and believable. If you cannot measure the outcome, describe what changed for the people doing the work.

Make bullets concrete

A useful bullet starts with an action and gives enough context to understand why it mattered. “Built a weather app” is a label. “Built a responsive weather dashboard that compares a saved city with local conditions using a public API” gives the reader a clearer picture. Add a result if you have one, but do not invent numbers just because resume advice says metrics are powerful.

Use the language of the job description where it accurately describes your work. If the role asks for SQL and your project uses SQL, name it. If it asks for production experience and you have only completed a tutorial, do not imply otherwise. Search systems and human reviewers both benefit from words that are precise. Keyword repetition can make a resume harder to trust.

A retail worker might write: “Resolved customer issues at the returns desk and documented repeat defects for the shift lead.” Add a real count if you have one. Do not invent a percentage.

Give the reader a sensible order

For an early-career candidate, a one-page resume is often enough. Put your name and contact details first, then a short summary only if it adds useful context, followed by skills, projects, experience, and education. If you are changing careers, relevant experience can appear above education. The first half of the page should quickly answer what kind of role you want and what evidence supports the application.

Use a simple layout with readable type and familiar headings. Decorative columns, icons, and progress bars can break when a resume is parsed or printed. Save a PDF unless the application requests another format, then open the PDF and check that text selects in the right order. The file name should identify you and the role, not say “final_final.”

Translate experience from another field

Previous jobs can be relevant even when they were not in technology. A teacher may have built a system for tracking student progress. A retail worker may have spotted recurring inventory errors and improved the handoff. A support agent may have learned to reproduce a bug from incomplete reports. Describe these tasks in terms of the problem, your action, and the result without pretending they were software engineering responsibilities.

A career change does not erase your prior expertise. It changes which parts need emphasis. Keep the details that show communication, careful execution, collaboration, or domain knowledge related to the role you want. Reduce unrelated duties that take space but do not help a reader understand your fit.

Read it like a skeptical hiring manager

For every claim, ask: can I point to proof? “Excellent problem solver” is hard to verify. A bullet about tracing a recurring data mismatch to a timezone conversion is much easier to discuss. Ask a friend to spend thirty seconds with the page and tell you what role they think you are pursuing. If they cannot tell, the summary and first project need sharper framing.

Tailor a few lines for each application rather than rebuilding the document from scratch. Move the most relevant project higher, use the employer’s accurate terminology, and remove details that distract. Keep a master document with all your evidence so tailoring stays honest and quick.

Frequently asked questions

How long should an entry-level tech resume be?

One page is a useful target when you are early in your career. Use the space for clear evidence rather than shrinking the type or adding unrelated details.

Should I list skills I only studied in a course?

You can list tools you have used, but describe your level accurately. Be ready to explain where you used each one and what you can do without step-by-step instructions.

Do recruiters reject resumes without a degree?

Requirements vary by employer and role. A degree can matter for some positions, while others emphasize experience, projects, or demonstrated skills. Read the actual listing and show relevant evidence.

Should I include a summary statement?

Include one if it clearly says what role you want and what relevant evidence you bring. Skip generic claims that could appear on any applicant’s resume.
