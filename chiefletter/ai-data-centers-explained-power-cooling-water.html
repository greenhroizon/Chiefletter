AI data centers explained: power, cooling and water

Training and running AI models takes enormous computing capacity. Here is how data centers are built and why communities are debating them.

What is inside a data center

A data center is a facility filled with servers, storage and networking equipment, plus the systems that supply power and remove heat. AI workloads rely heavily on accelerators such as graphics processing units, which draw far more power per rack than traditional servers.

Power

Electricity is the largest operating requirement. Operators connect to the grid, sign long-term power agreements, and increasingly consider on-site generation. Because large facilities can strain local infrastructure, regulators and utilities often study who pays for new lines and plants.

Cooling and water

Almost all electricity used by computing turns into heat, which must be removed. Methods include air cooling, evaporative cooling that uses water, and liquid cooling that circulates fluid close to the chips. Air-cooled designs use less water but can need more electricity; the best choice depends on climate and local water supply.

Why communities push back

Residents ask about noise, land use, water consumption, grid costs and the number of permanent jobs created after construction ends. Developers respond with community agreements, efficiency improvements and commitments to cover their own energy costs. Our recent news coverage shows these debates reaching ballot boxes.

What to watch

  • Efficiency gains in chips and cooling.
  • New power sources built alongside data centers.
  • Transparent reporting of water and energy use.
  • Local rules on permitting and tax incentives.

Reading a data center announcement critically

When a company announces a large data center, a few questions reveal how significant it is. How many megawatts of power will it use, and where will the electricity come from? How will it be cooled, and how much water is needed? Who pays for new transmission lines and power plants? How many permanent jobs will it create after construction ends?

Also look at the timeline and the commitments. Are the numbers firm or targets? Are community agreements public? Independent reporting and local government documents often add details that press releases leave out. Comparing these answers across projects shows why some data centers are welcomed and others face opposition, and it explains why engineers are working on more efficient chips, new cooling methods and on-site power.

Frequently asked questions

Why do AI data centers need so much power?

Accelerators run many calculations at once and are packed densely, so each rack draws much more electricity than traditional servers.

Do all data centers use a lot of water?

No. Air-cooled and closed-loop designs use little water, though they can trade that for more electricity.

Who pays for new power infrastructure?

It varies by region and agreement, which is why regulators examine cost allocation.

Can data centers use renewable energy?

Many buy renewable power or build their own generation, but matching supply to constant demand is challenging.

Key terms

Rack
A cabinet holding servers.
PUE
Power usage effectiveness, a measure of facility efficiency.
Liquid cooling
Using fluid to carry heat away from chips.
The bottom line. AI infrastructure is a power and planning story as much as a chip story. Efficiency and transparency will shape how welcome it is.
