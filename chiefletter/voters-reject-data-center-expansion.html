Voters turn against data centers: 40 of 41 local ballot measures go against them

Ballotpedia's count, cited by Reuters, shows a sharp public backlash against AI infrastructure build-outs.

By ChiefLetter StaffPublished Nov 7, 2026Updated Nov 12, 20263 min read

Key points

  • In the US midterms, 40 of 41 data-center-related local ballot measures went against the projects, per Ballotpedia figures cited by Reuters.
  • Ohio had the most measures, with nearly 20 towns, cities and counties voting.
  • Voters cited water, electricity bills and secrecy in deals between local governments and developers.
  • Some measures are advisory, so their practical effect varies from place to place.

In the US midterm elections, 40 of 41 data-center-related ballot measures went against the projects, according to Ballotpedia figures cited by Reuters. Reporting on the preliminary results describes votes across roughly ten states, and the pattern was consistent: where voters were asked to restrict, review or ban data centers, they mostly said yes.

The one apparent win for the industry came in Harwood, North Dakota, where about 75% of the 430 voters rejected a charter amendment that would have required an independent review and public hearing before large industrial projects, including data centers, could be adopted. That amendment would not have affected the Applied Digital campus that had already been approved there.

Where the votes happened

Ohio had the largest number of contests, with almost 20 towns, cities and counties voting. Some asked whether to ban data centers outright; others asked about zoning changes. Opponents appear to have won every one. The results follow a separate statewide effort to put a data center construction ban amendment before Ohio voters, possibly in 2027.

In Emporia, Kansas, voters passed a prohibition on "high impact data centers" with just over 60% of the vote. The definition covers facilities with a peak electrical load above 10 megawatts or a daily water draw above 100,000 gallons, and it may also reach AI training facilities and crypto mining operations. Alhambra, California, which was the only California jurisdiction with a question on the ballot, appears to have approved a citywide ban in preliminary results. Defeats for data center proposals were also reported in Illinois, Maine and New Jersey, and more votes are scheduled for next month in Arkansas and North Dakota.

Earlier this year, voters in Port Washington, Wisconsin backed requiring voter approval for tax increment financing districts above $10 million, and Monterey Park, California voters overwhelmingly approved a permanent ban.

Reuters also pointed to winners in Texas and Georgia who campaigned on slowing expansion. Some of the measures are advisory rather than binding, so local officials retain some discretion over what happens next.

Why voters said no

The concerns are practical and local. Residents point to the strain on electricity grids and the risk of higher power bills, heavy water use, the amount of land consumed, and the number of permanent jobs a facility actually provides once construction ends. Another recurring complaint is a lack of transparency: many deals between local governments and developers have been negotiated under nondisclosure agreements, leaving residents to learn the details after the fact.

NBC exit polling cited in coverage found that 56% of voters said AI and data centers were a crucial or major factor in their vote. Analysts quoted in the reporting described voter frustration with projects being imposed quickly and without guardrails, rather than a rejection of the technology itself.

What it means for the AI buildout

Technology companies are racing to secure computing capacity for AI services, and sites with available power, land and permissive zoning are increasingly scarce. A run of local defeats raises the cost and the timeline of finding them. It also strengthens the hand of communities negotiating for commitments on water, power pricing and tax revenue.

Some developers are already responding with air-cooled designs, pledges to pay the full cost of power, and community benefit programs. Google's Wyoming announcement the same week is one example. Whether such commitments are enough to change the outcome of future votes is the open question.

What to watch next

  • The December votes in Arkansas and North Dakota.
  • Whether a statewide data center ban reaches the Ohio ballot.
  • Court challenges or state-level preemption efforts aimed at overriding local restrictions.
  • More disclosure requirements for agreements between municipalities and developers.
