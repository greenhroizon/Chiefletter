How to spot phishing emails and text messages before you click

Phishing remains one of the most common ways accounts are compromised. Learn the warning signs and what to do when something looks off.

What phishing is

Phishing is an attempt to trick you into revealing passwords, payment details or one-time codes, or into installing malware. Messages imitate banks, delivery companies, employers and well-known brands. Attackers rely on urgency and fear because rushed people make mistakes.

Warning signs

  • Urgent threats, such as account closure or a missed payment, with a short deadline.
  • A sender address that looks close to the real one but has small differences.
  • Links whose visible text does not match the real destination when you hover over them.
  • Requests for passwords, codes or gift cards, which legitimate companies do not ask for by message.
  • Unexpected attachments, especially compressed files or documents asking you to enable macros.
  • Generic greetings combined with unusual wording or formatting.

What to do instead of clicking

Go to the company website by typing the address yourself or using a saved bookmark, then check your account there. If a coworker or relative seems to ask for money or credentials, confirm through another channel such as a phone call to a known number.

If you already clicked

Do not panic, but act quickly. Change the password for the affected account and any account that shared it, enable two-factor authentication, and sign out of other sessions. If you entered payment details, contact your bank. If it happened on a work device, report it to your IT or security team right away; early reports limit damage.

Building good habits

Slow down on messages that create pressure, use a password manager that only fills on genuine sites, and keep your devices updated. Reporting phishing to your email provider helps filters improve for everyone.

Three realistic scams and how to respond

Consider a text claiming a package could not be delivered and asking you to pay a small fee through a link. The amount is small, the message is urgent and the link goes to a look-alike page. The safe response is to ignore the link, open the carrier's real app or website yourself and check whether a delivery is actually pending. Then delete and report the text.

Now consider an email, apparently from your boss, asking you to buy gift cards urgently and send the codes. The address may be close to the real one, and the tone may copy your boss's style. Verify by calling or messaging your boss through a known channel. A third example is a security alert claiming your account was accessed from another country, with a button to secure it. Do not use the button; sign in through your usual bookmark and review recent activity there. In each case the same habit protects you: leave the message, go to the real source and decide calmly.

Frequently asked questions

Can a message from a known contact be phishing?

Yes. Accounts get hijacked, and attackers copy real messages. Confirm unusual requests through another channel.

Is it safe to open a suspicious email?

Opening is usually lower risk than clicking links or attachments, but delete it or report it.

What is smishing?

Phishing by text message, often claiming to be a delivery company or bank.

What is spear phishing?

A targeted attack that uses personal or company details to look believable.

Put the advice into practice

Treat urgency as a reason to slow down. Read the sender address, but do not rely on the display name: look for misspellings, extra words and a domain that is merely similar to the real one. On a computer, hover over a link to inspect its destination; on a phone, press and hold only if you know how to preview the URL safely. The simplest check is often to open the organization’s app or type its known address yourself rather than following the message.

  • Verify requests for money, passwords, gift cards or changed bank details through a second channel you already trust.
  • Do not reply to a suspicious text to ask whether it is real.
  • Report it through your email or messaging app, then delete it.
  • If you entered a password, change it directly on the service, revoke unknown sessions and enable a second factor.

Keep a short note of what you changed and what result you observed. That makes it easier to undo an unwanted side effect, compare options fairly and repeat the parts that worked. Revisit the setup when your devices, accounts or work change; a good technology routine is one you can explain, maintain and recover from.

Frequently asked questions

Can a message from someone I know be phishing?

Yes. Their account may be compromised or the sender name may be copied. Confirm an unusual request by calling or messaging the person through a separate channel you already use.

Is it safe just to open a suspicious email?

The greater risks usually come from links, attachments and replies, but remote content can reveal that an address is active. Use your mail app’s report function and avoid interacting with the message.

What should I do after clicking a link?

Close the page without entering information. If you submitted credentials, change them from the genuine site, sign out other sessions and tell your organization’s IT contact if it was a work account.

What is smishing?

It is phishing delivered by text message. Delivery alerts, toll notices and account warnings are common themes; verify them through the company’s official app or website.

Key terms

Phishing
Tricking people into giving up secrets.
Spoofing
Faking a sender's identity.
Malware
Software designed to cause harm.
The bottom line. Slow down, verify through a trusted channel and never share codes or passwords on request. A short pause defeats most phishing.
