How large language models work, explained in plain English

Chatbots can write, summarize and answer questions. Here is what is actually happening under the hood, and why they sometimes get things wrong.

Predicting the next piece of text

A large language model is a program trained to predict what text is likely to come next. During training, it reads enormous amounts of text and adjusts billions of internal numbers, called parameters, so its predictions improve. Text is split into small units called tokens, and the model works with patterns across those tokens rather than with a database of facts.

From prediction to conversation

Raw prediction alone does not make a good assistant. Developers further train models on examples of helpful answers and use human feedback to steer them toward being useful and safe. When you type a prompt, the model produces a reply one token at a time, each choice influenced by everything that came before it in the conversation.

Why mistakes happen

Because the model generates plausible text, it can state something fluent but false, often called a hallucination. It may also reflect biases in its training data, and it has a knowledge cutoff unless connected to search tools. Treat outputs as a draft to verify, especially for medical, legal, financial or factual claims.

Getting good results

  • Give clear context and the goal of the task.
  • Specify the format you want, such as a table or short summary.
  • Provide source text when accuracy matters and ask the model to stay within it.
  • Check important facts against reliable sources.

What they are good and bad at

Language models are strong at drafting, rewriting, summarizing, brainstorming and explaining code. They are weaker at precise arithmetic without tools, up-to-the-minute facts and tasks that require guaranteed correctness. Matching the tool to the job is the key to using them well.

Using a model for a real task: summarizing a long report

Say you need to summarize a 30-page report for your team. Pasting the text and typing 'summarize this' produces something generic. A better approach is to state the audience, the purpose and the format, for example: a half-page summary for managers, with three main findings, two risks and one recommended action, using only information in the text.

Then check the result. Compare each claim to the report, look for numbers that do not appear in the source, and ask the model to point to the section supporting each finding. If something seems wrong, say so and ask for a correction. This workflow shows both the strength and the limit of the technology: it is fast at condensing and structuring text, but you remain responsible for accuracy. For sensitive documents, use an approved tool whose data policy you understand rather than a public chatbot.

Frequently asked questions

Do language models understand language?

They model patterns in text very effectively and can seem to reason, but they do not have human experience, and they can still be wrong.

Why do they sometimes invent facts?

They generate likely text rather than looking up verified records unless connected to tools.

What is a token?

A small chunk of text, often part of a word, which models use as their basic unit.

Can models learn from my chats?

It depends on the provider's settings and terms, so check privacy options before sharing sensitive information.

Put the advice into practice

Treat a model response as a draft or hypothesis. Give it the task, audience, relevant context and constraints, then ask for a format you can check. For research, request the source material or references and open them yourself; a fluent citation can still be wrong. For code, run it in a safe test environment and inspect what it changes. For summaries, compare the result with the original document, especially names, numbers, dates and exceptions.

  • Do not paste private customer information, credentials or confidential business material into a service unless your organization has approved that use.
  • Check the product’s retention and training controls.
  • For important decisions, keep a human accountable for the final call and record the source documents used.
  • A model is most useful when it saves time on a bounded task and a person can judge the result.

Keep a short note of what you changed and what result you observed. That makes it easier to undo an unwanted side effect, compare options fairly and repeat the parts that worked. Revisit the setup when your devices, accounts or work change; a good technology routine is one you can explain, maintain and recover from.

Frequently asked questions

Why do models make things up?

They generate likely sequences of text rather than checking every statement against a live source. Provide trusted material and verify important claims independently.

Can I trust an answer that includes sources?

Use sources as leads, not proof. Open each one and confirm it supports the specific claim, and check that the link points to a real document.

Should I use a model for private work files?

Only if the service and your organization’s rules allow it. Review data handling and retention terms, and remove personal or confidential details when possible.

What is a good first use case?

Choose a repeatable, low-risk task with clear success criteria, such as restructuring notes or drafting a checklist. Review the output before relying on it.

Key terms

Parameter
An adjustable number learned during training.
Hallucination
A fluent but incorrect output.
Context window
The amount of text a model can consider at once.
The bottom line. Language models are powerful drafting and thinking aids, not oracles. Give them good context, then check what matters.
