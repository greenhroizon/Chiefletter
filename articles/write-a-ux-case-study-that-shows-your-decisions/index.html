Write a UX Case Study That Shows Your Decisions

Tell the story of a design project with enough context to understand your choices, without turning the case study into a wall of polished screens.

Tell a story about a real design question

A case study is not a scrapbook of screens. It should help a reader understand what problem you were asked to solve, what you learned, which options you considered, and why the final direction made sense. Begin with a one-paragraph summary: the user, the context, your role, and the outcome. If the project was speculative or a course exercise, say so clearly.

Readers do not need a dramatic “before and after” if the project did not have one. They need honesty about what you observed and what remains uncertain. A small redesign based on three usability sessions is more credible when presented as a limited study than as proof that every user now loves the product.

Explain the problem before showing the answer

Describe the user’s task and the friction they encountered. Support the description with research, support tickets, analytics, or a clearly labeled assumption. Explain who you spoke with, how you recruited participants, and what the method can and cannot tell you. A few focused observations can be valuable; a large claim without a method is difficult to trust.

Separate what you saw from what you inferred. “Two participants missed the save control” is an observation. “Users do not understand saving” is an interpretation. The distinction helps a reviewer follow your reasoning and see where another round of research would help.

Describe one observed obstacle, the change you tested, and what happened in the next session. Do not turn one person’s comment into a claim about every user.

Show the decisions and the alternatives

Include sketches, flows, or early versions only when they explain a decision. Show what you tried, where it failed, and what changed. A case study becomes more useful when it captures the trade-off: perhaps a confirmation dialog reduced accidental deletion but added friction to a frequent task. A design choice is interesting because it solves one problem while creating another consideration.

Describe collaboration honestly. Name the parts you owned and the parts made by teammates. If you inherited constraints from engineering, compliance, or brand systems, explain how you worked within them. A realistic project rarely belongs to one person alone; showing the collaboration makes your contribution clearer, not smaller.

Connect the final design to evidence

Present the final flow in context and explain why it addresses the original problem. If you tested the design, describe the task, who participated, and what changed. If you did not measure a product outcome, do not imply that the design improved a business metric. You can still explain what you would measure after launch, such as task completion, error rate, or support requests.

Use a few legible images with captions and alt text. Avoid tiny mockups that look impressive in a portfolio grid but cannot be inspected on a phone. Keep confidential details out of public work and follow the employer’s rules. You can describe a process at a high level without revealing private screens or customer data.

Close with learning, not a victory lap

End with what you would do next, what changed in your thinking, and the main limitation of the work. Perhaps the prototype worked for first-time users but needed testing with people who use assistive technology. Perhaps the research surfaced a larger organizational issue the feature alone could not solve. This kind of reflection demonstrates judgment.

Keep the case study concise enough to read and deep enough to discuss. A recruiter may skim the summary and images; a design lead may study your decisions. Use headings and links so both can find what they need. The goal is not to prove you never made a wrong turn. It is to show how you navigate one.

Frequently asked questions

How many UX case studies should a portfolio have?

A few carefully chosen studies are usually enough to show range and depth. Choose projects you can explain clearly and that fit the roles you want.

Can I use a school project in a UX portfolio?

Yes, label it as a school or speculative project and explain the constraints. Do not present assumptions as findings from real users.

How long should a UX case study be?

Long enough to explain the problem, process, decisions, and outcome. Use a concise summary and clear sections so readers can skim or explore.

Can I show work from my current employer?

Only if you have permission and can avoid confidential information. When in doubt, create a sanitized explanation or leave the work private.
